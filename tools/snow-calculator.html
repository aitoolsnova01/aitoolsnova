Why Snow Forecasts Are Harder Than Rain Forecasts

Snow depends on a narrow temperature band through the whole depth of the atmosphere, not just at ground level. A layer barely above freezing a few hundred metres up turns forecast snow into sleet or cold rain, which is why snow predictions change more often and more dramatically than rain predictions.

The other complication is the ratio between liquid and snow depth. Ten millimetres of water can fall as anything from six centimetres of heavy wet snow near freezing to twenty-five centimetres of dry powder in much colder air. Forecasts quoting depth are already making an assumption about that ratio, and it is frequently the largest source of error.

Elevation makes the same forecast wrong in both directions within a few kilometres. Valleys often see rain while hills a short drive away accumulate steadily, which is why a single town-level number can mislead anyone commuting across that boundary.

Using the Forecast for Roads, Roofs and Travel

For driving, the useful question is when the snow starts relative to traffic, not how much falls. Five centimetres arriving during the evening commute causes far more disruption than twenty centimetres overnight, because road treatment works far better before compaction than after.

Roof load is about weight, not depth. Wet snow can weigh three to four times as much per centimetre as dry powder, and rain falling onto existing snow is the dangerous combination because the snowpack absorbs it. If a warm, wet system follows a cold snowy one, that is the sequence worth watching.

For travel, build in a buffer rather than trying to time the gap. Airlines and rail operators cancel on forecast rather than on conditions, so disruption often begins hours before the first flake and continues after the roads are clear.
