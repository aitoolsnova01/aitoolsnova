Static Versus Dynamic, and Why It Decides Everything

A static QR code encodes the destination directly in the pattern. It works forever, needs no account and cannot be tracked or changed. A dynamic code encodes a redirect through the provider's server, which allows editing the destination and viewing scan statistics, but makes your code dependent on that company continuing to exist and continuing to offer the service free.

This is the single most common regret in the category. Printing ten thousand menus with a dynamic code from a free tool, then receiving an email that the free tier now expires after ninety days, turns marketing collateral into dead paper.

Rule of thumb: anything printed and permanent should be static, ideally pointing at a short URL on a domain you control. That gives you the editability of dynamic codes without depending on anyone else's redirect.

Making Codes That Scan Reliably in the Real World

Contrast and quiet zone do most of the work. Dark pattern on a light background, never the reverse, and leave a clear margin of roughly four modules on every side. Codes placed tight against a design element fail far more often than people realise.

Test at the real distance and size before printing. A code that scans on your monitor may not scan at two metres on a poster, and heavy logo overlays eat into the error correction you were relying on. Print one, walk away from it and try it on an older phone.

What Codes Should Point To, and Measuring Results

The destination matters more than the code. A QR code landing on a desktop-oriented homepage wastes the scan, because every single visitor arrived on a phone, standing up, with limited patience. Build a specific mobile page for the campaign and the conversion difference is substantial.

Use a URL you control as the intermediate step even for printed codes. Pointing the code at a short path on your own domain gives you the ability to change the destination later without reprinting, and it keeps the analytics in your own tools rather than a third party's dashboard.

Give people a reason to scan. A bare code on a poster performs poorly; the same code with a specific promise next to it performs several times better, because the scan is a small act of trust that needs an obvious payoff.

What to Look For When Choosing A qr code tool

  • Permanent, non-expiring codes. Some free generators expire codes or redirect through their own domain, which breaks printed material.
  • Static versus dynamic. Static codes work forever offline. Dynamic codes are editable but depend on the provider staying alive.
  • Vector export for print. A low-resolution PNG will not scan reliably when printed large.
  • Error correction level. Higher correction keeps a code scannable when a logo covers part of it.

Mistakes That Cost Businesses and marketers Time

Using a free dynamic code on printed material. If the service shuts down or paywalls it, every printed code stops working.

Making it too small or low contrast. Anything under about two centimetres printed, or low contrast, fails on many phones.

Never testing before printing. Test on both Android and iPhone, at the actual printed size, before ordering.

More Questions Answered

Do free QR codes expire?

Static codes never expire because the data is inside the image. Dynamic codes depend on the provider, and free plans sometimes expire or paywall them.

What is the difference between static and dynamic?

Static encodes the destination directly and cannot be changed. Dynamic points at a redirect the provider controls, so the destination is editable but dependent on their service.

Can I add a logo?

Yes, at high error correction, covering no more than roughly thirty percent of the code. Always test after adding one.

What size should a printed QR code be?

At least two by two centimetres for close scanning, and considerably larger for posters or signage.
